Rip it. Burn it.

Three jobs, each with the formats you would expect.

Rip a CD

In: any audio CD.
Out: MP3 files (variable or fixed quality, up to 320 kbps) or M4A files (AAC), named by song and filled with the album details and cover.

Burn a CD

In: a folder of MP3 or M4A files.
Out: a regular music CD (the standard audio CD format) that plays in any standard CD player, or an MP3 Disc, with folders if you like, for players that read MP3 files.

Burn a Video DVD

In: video files: MOV, MP4, M4V, AVI, MKV, WMV, MPG, WebM, and FLV.
Out: a standard DVD-Video disc with a menu Ripple draws for you. It plays in any DVD player.

Ripping a CD, in three steps

No setup screens and no file name templates to learn.

  1. Insert a disc

    Plug in a CD drive and insert an audio disc. Ripple shows its tracks right away.

  2. Check the details

    Ripple looks up the album, the artist, the track titles, and the cover. It tells you when a match is only a guess. Fix anything that looks wrong, or pick a different match.

  3. Rip

    Press Rip. Your files land in the folder you chose, in Artist/Album folders, named 01 - Song Title.mp3 (or .m4a).

What Ripple does

One window, a few good defaults.

Finds the album for you

Looks the disc up on MusicBrainz, a free community music database, and fetches the cover from the Cover Art Archive. If the disc is not an exact match, Ripple says so and ranks the candidates by how well their track lengths fit.

Names files by song title

Every file is named after its song. Characters that Macs do not allow in file names are swapped out, so nothing fails halfway.

Easy to correct

Edit the album, the artist, and any track title. Skip tracks you do not want. Drop your own picture on the cover. Cancel any time: finished tracks stay.

Handles tricky discs

Several albums match one disc? You choose. Two-disc sets go into Disc 1 and Disc 2 folders. Compilations put the artist in each file name. Discs with a data session after the music work too.

Details inside every file

Title, artist, album, year, track and disc numbers, and the cover are written into each file, so other players can show them.

MP3 or M4A

Choose MP3 (variable or fixed quality, up to 320 kbps) or M4A (AAC at 256 kbps). The choice is saved.

Checks for scratches

Ripple reads each part of the disc again to make sure the reads agree. A track it cannot read cleanly is still saved, marked Needs Attention, and you can rip just that track again.

Burns a regular music CD

Choose a folder of MP3 or M4A files and burn a standard Audio CD. It plays in any standard CD player, car stereo, or old boom box. Or burn an MP3 Disc, which holds many hours for players that read MP3 files. M4A files are converted to MP3 for you.

Makes a Video DVD with a menu

Drop in your videos, put them in order, and rename them. Ripple shows how much of the disc they use and how good the picture will be, draws a menu with a picture and a button for each video, and burns a DVD that plays in any DVD player. Copy-protected videos, such as store-bought or rented movies, cannot be read, and Ripple says so.

Private

No account and no tracking. The only thing Ripple sends over the internet is the album and cover lookup, and ripping works without it. Privacy and terms

When a disc matches more than one album, you choose.

Reissues and special editions share the same disc. Ripple picks the closest match first and lets you switch with one click. Anything you typed yourself stays put when you switch.

Burn a music CD that plays in any CD player.

Choose a folder of MP3 or M4A files, drag them into the order you want, and watch the capacity bar. Ripple turns them into a regular Audio CD, the standard format, so it plays in standard CD players, car stereos, and old boom boxes. Use a blank CD-R: many older players cannot read CD-RW discs. If the files do not fit, Ripple says how much to remove.

Prefer MP3 files on the disc? Burn an MP3 Disc instead. It holds many hours, for players that read MP3 files. Make folders on the disc, drop your files into them, and M4A files are converted to MP3 as it burns.

Make a Video DVD with a menu you can see first.

Drop your videos on the window. Ripple reads each one, shows its length, and keeps a bar of how much of the disc is used, with a word for the picture quality: Excellent, Good, or Reduced. If there is too much, it says how many minutes to remove. A dual-layer disc in the drive raises the room.

The Menu panel shows every page of the menu before anything is made. Pick a look (Classic, Midnight, Daylight, or Warm), choose your own background picture, and add music that plays under the menu. Videos over 10 minutes get chapters every 5 minutes and a page of scene pictures to jump to. Choose NTSC (US) or PAL (Europe), and Ripple fits each video onto the screen with bars, never stretching it. Then build the disc and burn it, or save it as a disc image to open in a DVD player app.

What to know about this early version

Ripple is new. Here is what has been tried, and what has not.

  • Ripping has been tried on real music CDs with one USB drive, including a disc with a data session. The audio matched the Mac’s own copy exactly.
  • Burning takes MP3 and M4A files. Other kinds of audio files are not accepted for burning yet. On an MP3 Disc, M4A files are converted to MP3 (192 kbps), so they lose a little quality.
  • Burning an MP3 Disc has been tried on a real disc and checked file by file. Burning an Audio CD has not been tried on a real CD-R yet.
  • Video DVDs are new. The menu, the conversion, and the disc image are tested on this Mac, but a Video DVD has not been tried on a real blank disc or in a DVD player yet. Converting is slow: a two-hour movie takes roughly 20 to 40 minutes on an Apple silicon Mac. Ripple cannot read copy-protected video and does not try to get around it. Version one leaves out subtitles, extra audio tracks, moving menus, and Blu-ray.
  • Check for errors catches reads that fail or disagree. It cannot catch a drive that repeats the same wrong data twice. Stronger checking is planned.

Ripple stands on other people’s work. Thank you to the people behind each of these.

dvdauthor and spumux
Write the DVD’s files and lay the menu buttons over the menu. GNU General Public License, version 2 or later. Separate programs inside Ripple.
LAME
Makes the MP3 files. GNU Lesser General Public License. A separate library inside Ripple that you can replace.
MusicBrainz and the Cover Art Archive
Find the album, the song titles, and the cover. The iTunes Search API is the fallback for covers.

The licences, version numbers, and where to get the source of the three programs and the library are in Ripple’s Resources/Licenses folder, and the source of each is available at the addresses above. These names belong to their owners, and Ripple is not affiliated with them.

Requirements

A Mac
With Apple silicon, running macOS 26 or later.
A disc drive
Most Macs have no disc drive. A USB CD or DVD drive works. Burning needs a drive that can write discs and a blank disc: a CD-R for a regular music CD, or a DVD-R or DVD-RW for a Video DVD.
Space on your Mac
A Video DVD is made in a work folder first. Ripple needs about twice the disc’s size, plus a little, free on your Mac while it works, and tells you before it starts if there is not enough.
Internet
Only for the album and cover lookup. You can type the details yourself without it.
